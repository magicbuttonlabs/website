Every leadership team has the same planning problem

By Brandon Huff & Ethan Foulkes June 2, 2026 5 min read

You replanned the same initiative three times this quarter and you’re still not sure it’s settled. Two teams each think the other one owns it. Something got marked “done” — done to engineering, anyway, not to leadership, and not to the team waiting downstream. And if someone asked you, right now, how the plan is actually tracking, you’d have to go around and ask.

If you just nodded at any of that: you’re not the outlier. We hear this on call after call — different industries, different company sizes, the same knot. It’s so consistent we have a name for it.

The universal planning problem

Across everything from enterprise agile transformations to highly structured manufacturing shops, the same cluster of complaints shows up. Whenever someone says they need to do “planning,” the honest follow-up is: planning at what level, for what purpose, for which team? — and that ambiguity is where it all starts to fray. The pattern is reliable enough that we treat it as a given, not a surprise.

The four symptoms

  • Constant replanning. You commit to something, then quietly unplan it, never quite sure it’s locked.
  • Ownership confusion. Which team owns it, at what level, at what detail — and what actually has to be delivered.
  • “Done” means different things. Finished for one team isn’t finished for management or the next team in line.
  • No idea how it’s going. There’s no shared place to see progress, so problems surface late, if at all.

They look like four problems. They’re really one, wearing four outfits.

It isn’t that your team is uniquely disorganized. It’s the default state of strategy that isn’t connected and isn’t visible.

Why it’s not your team’s fault

The instinct is to read these symptoms as a discipline failure — people aren’t updating things, teams aren’t communicating. But the root is structural. The plan lives in one place (a deck, a doc, an executive’s head), the work lives in another (a dozen team boards and tools), and nothing connects them. With no line from goals to the actual work, and no shared view of how it’s going, every one of those four symptoms is inevitable. It would happen to a great team too.

The good news

If it’s structural, it’s fixable — and the fix isn’t more planning discipline or yet another framework. It’s making the whole thing visible and connected, so strategy stays alive instead of fragmenting. We get into the how separately. For now, the thing worth hearing is the part you don’t usually get told: you’re not behind, and you’re not uniquely broken. You’re on the same call as almost everyone.

Key takeaways

  • The “universal planning problem” shows up across industries and company sizes — it’s the norm, not the exception.
  • Four symptoms: constant replanning, ownership confusion, “done” meaning different things, no visibility into progress.
  • They share one root: strategy and work that aren’t connected or visible.
  • It’s structural, not a discipline failure — which is exactly why it’s fixable.
